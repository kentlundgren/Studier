What does ”best” mean when the model writes the code?

A reading of a LinkedIn post claiming GPT‑6 had overtaken Claude – and an attempt to weigh coding ability, harness and price against each other without pretending there is a definitive answer.

Kent Lundgren · 9 September 2026 · figures as of September 2026

About the numbers

I have made my own assessment, 1–5 per facet, in the overview below. It is my judgement, not a measurement – and it can of course change as the evidence improves. The reasoning behind each number, with sources, is in the research notes, and the full cost arithmetic in the cost calculation (both in Swedish). A couple of subscription figures have not been checked against the companies' own help pages.

If you just want the summary: skip to the overview.

A post by Osama Romoh on LinkedIn (Romoh, 2026) caught my eye in my feed. The headline was roughly: ”I love Claude, but GPT‑6 Astra blew Fable 5 out of the water for me.” He had used Claude for a long time, was surprised by OpenAI's new model, and bought ChatGPT Pro alongside his Claude Max subscription. So he kept both.

I do a fair amount of coding with generative AI these days – small web pages and tools, this page is one example, and I have collected a few others in a presentation of my own (Lundgren, 2026b). How I usually work when I code with a model – from requirements document to published page – I have described in what I call the Claude compass (Lundgren, 2026a). That is where this comparison fits: the toolchain around it is the same regardless of model, and what I am weighing is which coding engine sits inside it.

I have settled on Claude Code. So Romoh's post nagged at me. Is GPT‑6 better? And what do we even mean by better, when the tool writes part of the code for us? That is the question I wanted to dig into, with my own view of knowledge as the lens.

What the post says #

Romoh's point is narrower than the headline. He does not write about code quality and he shows no numbers. What he is taken with is that GPT‑6 follows instructions more closely: ask for a short answer and you get a short answer, not five paragraphs explaining why the answer is short. ”Astra has been really, really good at giving me what I actually asked for”, he writes, adding that it matters when you use the models for real work and do not want to keep reminding them of instructions you have already given.

It is a subjective working impression on a single dimension. But it works as a starting point, because it opens the three questions I find interesting: can it code, how do you steer it, and what does the value cost. Before that, one thing that made the whole comparison trickier than I expected.

Which model are we even talking about? #

When I set out to find what was actually the case, I noticed that the question ”which model” has no simple answer, and that this is itself part of the problem.

The models do not stand still. The companies upgrade often, and every upgrade gets a new name or number. On the Claude side there are names and numbers side by side right now: Fable 5.1, Mythos 5.1, Opus 5 (with 4.8, 4.7, 4.6, 4.5 still in the price list), Sonnet 5, Haiku 4.5. On the OpenAI side there is GPT‑6 Astra (released 3 September 2026), three variants of GPT‑5.6 called Sol, Terra and Luna, and the previous flagship 5.5. A code-specific model, 5.3‑codex, has already been retired. GPT‑5.4 was retired on 31 August, and if you had it saved in a configuration file you had to swap it for 5.6‑terra yourself.

The names also hide what is running. ”GPT‑6”, ”Claude Code” and ”Codex” are not models but families or products. The screenshot in front of me said ”GPT‑6 Astra Low” – that is model, variant and effort level stacked into one label. And which underlying model actually answers can be swapped or re-routed without my noticing.

So every figure in this text is dated ”as of September 2026”, and ”which model is actually being used” is not a footnote but part of the answer. It reminds me of something I keep coming back to about tests and grades: the measure is a choice, not a law of nature – and here even the thing being measured changes its name faster than you can get used to it.

Can it code? #

Short answer: both can, roughly equally well, and the benchmarks are having a hard time telling them apart any more.

It is usually measured with tests like SWE‑bench Verified (solve real GitHub issues) and Terminal‑Bench (handle messy terminal tasks). An independent index from Artificial Analysis, published the same day GPT‑6 came out (Artificial Analysis, 2026), puts Claude Fable 5.1 in Claude Code at 70 and GPT‑6 Astra at 67 – a tie in practice, and Opus 5 is at the same level. On another agentic coding test, DeepSWE, GPT‑6 is described as ”a tie, not a takeover”. Where Astra wins clearly is on Terminal‑Bench 4.0, that is, long, messy terminal work with error recovery.

A sign of how blunt the measuring stick has become: when Anthropic presented Opus 5 (Anthropic, 2026a) they did not even mention SWE‑bench Verified, leading instead with other tests and with cost per task. The top models are so close that the test is saturated. And Terminal‑Bench exists in at least three versions at once, with different winners. Which test you pick is itself a position.

There is a faint quality edge in Claude's favour. In Prathit Joshi's review, where he ran both for over a hundred hours (Joshi, 2026), blind reviewers rated Claude's code cleaner in two cases out of three, even when he himself preferred Codex day to day. But that is one voice, not a trend, and I do not read more into it than that.

How do you steer it? #

This – harness, how the model is connected to your files, tools and instructions – is to my mind the most interesting dimension, and the one Romoh is really onto. I split it in two: what is there, and how it feels.

What is there #

Claude Code (Anthropic, 2026b) reads a CLAUDE.md in the project at the start of every session, builds its own memory between sessions, can be extended with ”skills” and ”hooks” (shell commands before or after an action), connects to external tools via MCP, and runs several sub-agents in parallel. It is available in the terminal, in VS Code and Cursor, in JetBrains, in a standalone desktop app and in the browser – and the same CLAUDE.md and the same settings apply everywhere.

Codex (OpenAI, 2026b) reads an AGENTS.md with layered overrides, has configuration files, supports MCP, and now shares the same ”skills” standard as Claude. It is available as an open-source CLI, an IDE extension, a cloud service, in the ChatGPT app and on the web.

In other words: the mechanics are converging. A year ago the difference was clearer; now both support the same tool standard and the same connection protocol. Claude still has more documented primitives that are its own – the automatic memory, hooks – but the gap has narrowed.

How it feels #

Here there is a genuine dividing line, and it is about philosophy more than features. Claude Code is interactive: it shows its reasoning and asks at decision points. Codex leans towards the autonomous: you hand over a task and get back an implementation. The difference is usually described roughly like this: Claude Code is like a colleague who keeps telling you what it is doing; Codex is more ”hand over a task, get the solution back”. Neither is wrong – it depends on what you want.

You can see it in the apps. Here are the two, side by side (click for a larger image; the screenshots show the Swedish interface):

Both apps by now have projects, scheduled tasks, plugins and cloud execution. The difference is what they foreground. And for me it matters: I use Cursor to handle Git and GitHub regardless of model, so what I am comparing is which coding engine I run alongside Cursor. A CLI or an IDE extension that works against the same local files fits that chain better than an app with its own, more walled-off project space.

What does the value cost? #

My rule of thumb is simple: if a model costs twice as much it should be twice as good, otherwise it is worse value – quality is value divided by cost. It forces the question of how much capacity I actually need.

The flagships are priced identically: both Claude Fable 5.1 and GPT‑6 Astra cost 10 dollars per million input tokens and 50 dollars per million output tokens (Anthropic, 2026c; OpenAI, 2026a). Three things break the symmetry anyway. Claude's newer models use a tokenizer that produces about 30 % more tokens for the same text, so the same task costs more than the list price suggests. Astra doubles the price at long context; Fable does not. And Astra is a step up in price from the previous generation – but on the other hand uses fewer tokens per coding task, which partly offsets it.

Go down a level – to ”good but not best”, that is Claude Sonnet 5 vs GPT‑5.6 Terra – and it gets cheaper and nearly even: about 2 dollars per million input and 10–12 per million output for both. And it is worth pausing on, because it seems to hold generally: the flagship costs about five times as much per token as the mid tier, for a difference in coding ability the benchmarks can barely measure. The second-best model is rarely much worse – but almost always much cheaper.

Working from a full-time scenario – 35 hours of coding a week (five a day, every day of the week), about 150 active hours a month – with a written-out assumption about how many tokens an active hour uses (just over two million, mostly cheap cache reads): raw API cost lands at an unreasonable level, around 10,000 kronor (SEK) a month for the flagship. Nobody pays that. You run a subscription and hit the limits. And here is how it looks, as far as I could verify it against tests and user reports not against the companies' own help pages:

  • With full-time use of the flagship you blow through the Claude subscription's weekly limits and pay expensive API metering for the excess – maybe 7,000 kronor a month. ChatGPT/Codex is described as rarely hitting limits even under heavy use – maybe 2,300 kronor. Anthropic has raised Claude Code's limits several times during 2026, which says something about them having been a problem.
  • With a mid-tier model that fits within the subscription it is about 1,000–2,000 kronor a month for both.

An observation that simplified the arithmetic: harness sits in the client, not in the model. If I switch from Fable to Sonnet I am still in the same Claude Code with the same CLAUDE.md. So it is only coding ability and cost that change when you switch model tier – not how it feels to steer.

Here is how I think about the three assessments, and where the reasoning is in full (the research notes, in Swedish):

  • Coding ability, 3 vs 3. The independent index reads 70 to 67, DeepSWE is called ”a tie”, and Codex wins Terminal‑Bench 4.0. None of the measurements gives a two-source basis for putting either above the other.
  • Harness – what is there, 4 vs 3. Claude Code's own documentation lists more primitives that are its own (automatic memory, hooks, Agent SDK) on top of what both now share. It is the only number I have put above 3, and the one I am least sure of.
  • Harness – how it feels, 3 vs 3. A real difference in style, but fit rather than better or worse. My own leaning is towards the interactive one; a referenced survey says most people prefer Codex day to day.

The story the table lands on: capability is roughly even, and price decides. If you require the flagship and run full-time, Codex gives close to three times more value per krona. If you settle for a good mid-tier model and stay within the subscription it is nearly even, with a hair's-breadth edge to Claude – because then the harness advantage outweighs the small price difference.

Where I land #

I am not going to switch away from Claude Code. But not because it is ”best” in any absolute sense – rather because the interactive style, the model showing how it thinks and asking at decision points, suits how I want to work. It is the same reason I am liberal about using AI when I am learning something: the difference that matters is between a tool that explains until I understand and one that delivers so I do not have to. A model that reasons out loud invites the first.

And my view of knowledge makes me suspicious of the benchmark contest itself. A test for code carries a view of knowledge just as a national exam does: it decides what counts as knowing. When Anthropic stops leading with SWE‑bench because it is saturated, and OpenAI wins a different measurement instead, that says more about which test you pick than about which model is best. ”Best” is a moving target, not least because the thing being measured changes its name faster than you can get used to it.

The question ”twice as expensive, twice as good?” has a clearer answer, and it is maybe the text's most useful conclusion: for most of what I do I do not need the flagship. The second-best model – Sonnet 5 or GPT‑5.6 Terra – is barely worse at what I ask of it, but costs about a fifth. And then the choice between Claude and Codex is almost a matter of taste – what separates them is how you want the tool to behave, not whether it can do the job.

What Romoh's post really showed was maybe not that GPT‑6 is better, but that a seasoned Claude user chose to pay for both. The answer was ”yes please”, not ”either/or”. It is a picture of the question, not a verdict.

How this page is built

A short note on the technology behind the page and on how it came about.

The technology

Three files, no framework

Plain HTML, CSS and JavaScript in three separate files. No React, no build chain, no external libraries. The fonts are the ones already in your browser.

Nothing leaves the page

No server, no analytics, no cookies, no login. JavaScript does four things: collapses the reference list, opens this box, drives the rotating panel of voices, and enlarges the app images on click.

Works without JavaScript

The whole text, the table and every source link work with scripts turned off too. Then the reference list stays open, the panel's three quotes show as a list, and a click on an app image opens the image file in the browser rather than in an overlay.

Built to be linked

Every section and every reference has a stable id, so a single part can be linked to directly – for example …/Nr2/index_eng.html#Overview.

The table scrolls sideways

On a narrow screen the overview table can be scrolled sideways in its own box, without the whole page doing so.

Two screenshots

The images of the two apps are my own, taken 9 September 2026. The left panel is kept on purpose – it shows what the apps actually look like in use. They show the Swedish interface. Click an image for a larger version; the caption links to where each app is downloaded.
